# Compose, Don't Fork: Engineering Brain Blueprint

Nothing on GitHub does what "Engineering Brain" needs end to end, and the evidence favors building a thin custom platform on roughly eight mature open components rather than forking any existing RAG product. The two closest systems, **Tencent WeKnora** (MIT, 34.9k stars, hybrid search, four-role RBAC, an editable AI-generated wiki with diff and rollback) and **RAGFlow** (Apache-2.0, 92.5k stars, the strongest open PDF table/OCR parser), cover ingestion, chat and a generated wiki, but neither has a compliance-checking or requirement-authoring engine, and bolting those on means forking large Go codebases whose search tiers (Elasticsearch, MySQL, MinIO) you do not otherwise want. The architecture the research supports is: **one PostgreSQL as system of record** (pgvector/pgvectorscale for ANN, ParadeDB or tsvector for BM25, Hatchet for job queues, access control as a SQL `WHERE` clause); **markdown-with-frontmatter as the canonical wiki store** with a Tiptap/Hocuspocus web editor on top and a review queue in front of every AI draft; **GPU-hosted expert-VLM parsers** (MinerU2.5 or PaddleOCR-VL) with Docling as the structured document model; **self-hosted Qwen3 embeddings and rerankers on TEI**; and generation via a hosted API with zero-data-retention terms or a vLLM/SGLang box, behind a thin typed API with no orchestration framework. **A dedicated vector database is not required**: at the realistic scale of 0.5–5 million chunks the vectors fit in 2–20 GB of RAM and ANN search is single-digit milliseconds on CPU; GPUs pay off on PDF parsing (7–14x), bulk embedding (10–100x), cross-encoder reranking (~30x) and LLM generation, not on search. **Obsidian remains a good power-user client but cannot be the team system of record** because it has no headless API, no permissions and no server-side concurrency, and no file-based tool has filled that gap, which is exactly why the "files canonical, web editor on top" hybrid is the recommendation. Compliance checking should treat the LLM as an extractor and matcher wrapped in deterministic numeric and unit comparison with an explicit "insufficient evidence" verdict, because independent studies put single-pass LLM verdicts at roughly **60–75% precision and 70–85% recall**, with missed non-compliance as the dominant failure. The largest open risks are the license terms of standards bodies (ISO, IEEE, ASHRAE now explicitly prohibit AI processing of their text), the absence of any engineering-specific retrieval benchmark, and the fact that every candidate platform's scale claim is self-reported.

## No existing project covers the brief, but three cover about half of it

The survey of open-source document-chat platforms found a crowded field in which the leaders have converged on the same retrieval recipe (hybrid keyword plus vector, a reranker, cited answers) but none has the shape of the requested product. The table below condenses the state of the strongest candidates as of 21 October 2026, taken from their GitHub pages, release notes and license files.

WeKnora is the only candidate that already combines a permissive license, hybrid retrieval with reranking, role-based access, OIDC, a large REST API, an editable wiki and an explicit tens-of-thousands-of-documents claim; its README describes "Keyword + vector hybrid search, rerank, parent-child chunking and GraphRAG", a Wiki mode with "in-browser editing, revision diff and rollback", and a v0.5.2 note that "Wiki ingest scales to 40k-document KBs" ([WeKnora](https://github.com/Tencent/WeKnora)). The risk is velocity: it went from v0.5.2 to v0.8.3 within months and is Tencent-driven, so expect upgrade churn. RAGFlow's strength is the parsing layer: DeepDoc does "layout analysis, OCR, and table recognition on CPU", recall is "paired with fused re-ranking", citations are traceable and a chunk view exists "for human intervention", with a Knowledge Compilation feature that emits "Wikis, Graphs, Trees, PageIndex, Mind Maps, Timelines, and Skills" ([RAGFlow](https://github.com/infiniflow/ragflow)). Its 1.0 rewrite in Go landed as release candidates in September and October 2026 ([releases](https://github.com/infiniflow/ragflow/releases)), and its issue tracker documents the operational cost of that parsing power: a 1,600-page, 5 MB PDF exhausted a **62 GB** task executor ([#11822](https://github.com/infiniflow/ragflow/issues/11822)), an 875-page PDF consumed 32 GB with knowledge-graph chunking ([#4668](https://github.com/infiniflow/ragflow/issues/4668)), and a 68-page PDF with GraphRAG took about three hours ([#5088](https://github.com/infiniflow/ragflow/issues/5088)). Onyx is the most enterprise-proven (its compose file runs FastAPI, Next.js, Celery workers, Postgres, Redis, two model servers and `opensearchproject/opensearch:3.6.0` ([compose](https://github.com/onyx-dot-app/onyx/blob/main/deployment/docker_compose/docker-compose.yml))), but everything under its `ee/` directories "may only be used in production" with a paid Enterprise License, and that is where SSO, SCIM and RBAC live ([backend/ee/LICENSE](https://github.com/onyx-dot-app/onyx/blob/main/backend/ee/LICENSE); [README](https://github.com/onyx-dot-app/onyx/blob/main/README.md)). Open WebUI is the most popular but structurally wrong for this corpus: a user with 60,000 files saw a 43–50 MB `/api/models` payload freeze the browser, and another at 30,000 documents saw the browser consume about 5 GB of RAM, with no maintainer fix in the thread ([discussion #13891](https://github.com/open-webui/open-webui/discussions/13891)); its license also prohibits removing "Open WebUI" branding once you exceed fifty users in a rolling 30-day window ([LICENSE](https://github.com/open-webui/open-webui/blob/main/LICENSE)). Dify's license forbids operating "a multi-tenant environment" without written authorization and forbids removing the logo ([Dify LICENSE](https://github.com/langgenius/dify/blob/main/LICENSE)), and one production team reported that its built-in RAG "directly led to suboptimal recall quality", which they fixed by routing retrieval to a separate engine ([Higress](https://higress.ai/en/blog/释放-rag-潜能-用-ai-网关帮助-dify-应用-开外挂)).

The "LLM wiki" half of the brief has its own small ecosystem, triggered by Karpathy's April 2026 idea file, which proposes immutable raw sources, an LLM-owned markdown wiki with `index.md` and an append-only `log.md`, and a schema file the human and model co-evolve; he scopes the index-only approach to "~100 sources, ~hundreds of pages" and says larger wikis "may need proper search tooling" ([Karpathy gist](https://gist.github.com/karpathy/442a6bf555914893e9891c11519de94f)). The most complete open implementations are **nashsu/llm_wiki** (GPL-3.0, 20.8k stars, Tauri desktop app, two-step LLM ingest, SHA-256 change cache, review items, `sources:` frontmatter on every page, but single-user) ([llm_wiki](https://github.com/nashsu/llm_wiki)) and **krishddd/llm-wiki** (MIT, FastAPI, SQLite graph plus BM25 plus Chroma, staged edits in `wiki/review/edits/`, bi-temporal facts with `valid_from`/`valid_to`/`superseded_by`, a contradiction detector that auto-resolves only above a 0.2 score margin, and pages scoring below 0.60 routed to human review) ([krishddd/llm-wiki](https://github.com/krishddd/llm-wiki)). Stanford's **STORM/Co-STORM** (MIT) can synthesize cited encyclopedia-style articles grounded on your own documents via its VectorRM retriever, but its authors say it "cannot produce publication-ready articles" ([stanford-oval/storm](https://github.com/stanford-oval/storm)). Neither llm_wiki nor krishddd is engineering-specific, and no project was found that ingests documents into an existing wiki engine with bidirectional sync.

For the compliance and requirements half, the market gap is real. Requirements-management vendors ship authoring-quality AI, not document-versus-spec verdicts: Jama Connect Advisor scores text against INCOSE rules and EARS notation with colour bands at 81–100%, 61–80% and 0–60% ([Jama help](https://help.jamasoftware.com/ah/en/jama-connect-advisor-/interpreting-the-results.html)); Siemens Polarion 2512 added a paid "Copilot" with INCOSE content validation, similarity analysis and a consistency check across linked work items, bring-your-own Azure OpenAI ([Polarion 2512](https://blogs.sw.siemens.com/polarion/polarion-x-2512-whats-new-and-noteworthy/)). The clause-level "supplier document against spec" job is served by construction submittal agents (Datagrid, explicitly positioned as pre-screening that "does not make approval decisions or replace the PE's stamp" ([Datagrid](https://www.datagrid.com/agents/summary-spec-submittal-agent))) and by hobby-scale open source: **epifaneia/ctsm** reads a customer's Polarion ReqIF export plus supplier PDFs and writes verdicts back into the same ReqIF with per-requirement status, rationale and page citations, verifies every quoted span against the source, keeps unverified verdicts "open" for a human, and reports zero false "compliant" verdicts on a nine-requirement STM32 test set ([ctsm](https://github.com/epifaneia/ctsm)); **Aaryan-Chokshi/CompChecker** produces a one-row-per-obligation matrix with a five-valued rating including `insufficient_evidence`, runs reverse checks so omitted obligations surface, and emits a supplier clarification sheet ([CompChecker](https://github.com/Aaryan-Chokshi/CompChecker)). Both have zero stars and no accuracy claims beyond the toy set; they are design references, not bases. For the requirement-store backbone, **StrictDoc** (Apache-2.0, releases 0.30.1–0.30.3 in September–October 2026, ReqIF and Excel import/export, UIDs and relations, web UI) is the natural choice ([StrictDoc](https://github.com/strictdoc-project/strictdoc); [releases](https://github.com/strictdoc-project/strictdoc/releases)), with the companion `reqif` parsing library ([reqif](https://github.com/strictdoc-project/reqif)), Doorstop's YAML-in-git as the simpler alternative ([Doorstop](https://github.com/doorstop-dev/doorstop)) and Sphinx-Needs' versioned `needs.json` exchange as a second interchange target ([sphinx-needs releases](https://github.com/useblocks/sphinx-needs/releases)).

Several well-known names should be ruled out. Weaviate's Verba was archived on 8 June 2026 ([Verba](https://github.com/weaviate/Verba)); R2R has had no commit since 7 November 2025 ([R2R commits](https://github.com/SciPhi-AI/R2R/commits/main)); Kotaemon made four commits in all of 2026 and carries an open CVE ([Kotaemon](https://github.com/Cinnamon/kotaemon)); Microsoft GraphRAG is "largely in maintenance mode" ([GraphRAG](https://github.com/microsoft/graphrag)); Quivr pivoted and says "Do not run it in production yet" ([Quivr](https://github.com/QuivrHQ/quivr)); Morphik is Business Source License 1.1 ([Morphik](https://github.com/morphik-org/morphik-core)); and MinIO, which several of these platforms depend on, archived its community repository on 25 April 2026 with no further binaries ([minio/minio](https://github.com/minio/minio)).

## Obsidian stays a client; markdown stays canonical, with a web editor on top

The user's Obsidian question has a clear answer: nothing has replaced it as a structured-markdown database for individuals, and nothing file-based has become a team system of record either. Obsidian itself has become more attractive commercially, since February 2025 "the Obsidian Commercial license is optional" and anyone can use it for work ([Obsidian blog](https://obsidian.md/blog/free-for-work/)). Its architecture is the blocker: the official CLI that shipped in v1.12 "is not headless. If the app isn't open, your first command launches it" ([CLI guide](https://www.dsebastien.net/the-complete-guide-to-the-obsidian-cli-everything-you-can-do-from-the-terminal/)), so every server-side workaround runs an Electron instance on a virtual display. Real-time multiplayer exists only through the Relay plugin, whose relay server is MIT but whose login, permissions and billing server is proprietary; "anyone with the share key can join", the free tier is three users, and RBAC and SSO start at $6 per user per month ([Relay](https://github.com/No-Instructions/Relay)). The would-be successors fare no better for a multi-user engineering team: Logseq's DB version is in beta with a "data loss is possible" warning and alpha real-time collaboration ([Logseq](https://github.com/logseq/logseq)); SiYuan is explicitly "One workspace, one writer" ([SiYuan](https://github.com/siyuan-note/siyuan)); Trilium describes itself as for personal knowledge bases ([Trilium](https://github.com/TriliumNext/Trilium)); Dendron is in maintenance only ([Dendron](https://github.com/dendronhq/dendron)); Foam is alpha-grade ([Foam](https://github.com/foambubble/foam)). SilverBullet (MIT, Rust server, files on disk, HTTP space API, built-in object query language) is the strongest file-based web front end but documents no page-level permissions or real-time co-editing ([SilverBullet](https://github.com/silverbulletmd/silverbullet)). Obsidian Bases could not be evaluated because the help site was unreachable; treat that as unresearched.

The reason to keep markdown-plus-frontmatter as the canonical store is that every working LLM-wiki implementation uses it. Karpathy's gist, llmwiki, second-brain, Auto LLM Wiki, MindBase and krishddd all operate on plain files with frontmatter and wikilinks, use git or an append-only log as history, and edit by exact text replacement, because agents can read, grep, diff and rewrite files natively and the schema travels with the repository ([Karpathy gist](https://gist.github.com/karpathy/442a6bf555914893e9891c11519de94f); [llmwiki](https://github.com/lucasastorian/llmwiki)). The reason not to make git the human interface is equally clear: the docs-as-code literature itself concedes that non-developer reviewers "still need a practical way to review docs changes without becoming Git users" ([Mintlify](https://www.mintlify.com/library/documentation-governance-with-git-and-branch-protection)), and critics of the LLM-wiki pattern flag "no RBAC, no ACID transactions, no concurrency controls" as the enterprise gap ([Towards AI](https://pub.towardsai.net/andrej-karpathy-killed-rag-or-did-he-the-llm-wiki-pattern-7824d876e790)). Fluid, electrical, civil and structural engineers need a Notion-style editor, live co-editing so two people never fight over a page, inline comments and a page tree.

Among ready-made web wikis, **Outline** has the best editor and the only first-party, actively maintained agent surface: v1.10.0 made `documents.update` accept an optional `lastRevision` for optimistic concurrency, made all MCP tools take and return Markdown, and added PDF and web import; v1.11.0 (12 October 2026) exposed skills over MCP ([Outline releases](https://github.com/outline/outline/releases)). Its license is Business Source License 1.1, which forbids only offering it as a hosted "Document Service" to third parties and converts to Apache-2.0 on a date that each release pushes out four years ([Outline LICENSE](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/outline/outline/main/LICENSE)); for an internal engineering wiki that is acceptable, but it is not open source and it requires an external identity provider. **Docmost** is AGPL at the core but its AI answers, MCP OAuth and vector search are enterprise-licensed ([Docmost releases](https://github.com/docmost/docmost/releases)). **BookStack** is MIT with a stable REST API whose page create/update calls now accept a changelog message, but it has no real-time co-editing ([BookStack releases](https://github.com/BookStackApp/BookStack/releases)). **XWiki** is the only wiki with a first-party RAG/LLM extension including an MCP server, still beta ([XWiki LLM Application](https://extensions.xwiki.org/xwiki/bin/view/Extension/LLM/LLM%20Application/)). Wiki.js 3.0 has been "NOT FOR PRODUCTION USE" beta for five years ([Wiki.js](https://github.com/requarks/wiki)).

The recommendation is therefore a hybrid rather than a pick. Keep a git repository of markdown with YAML frontmatter (`status`, `sources`, `generated_by`, `reviewed_by`, `base_revision`, `human_edited`) as the store that agents write to, and build the human editor on **Tiptap + Hocuspocus**: Tiptap's core is MIT, headless and ProseMirror-based, and Hocuspocus is an MIT Yjs WebSocket backend with SQLite, Redis and S3 persistence extensions that works with Tiptap, BlockNote or Lexical ([Tiptap](https://github.com/ueberdosis/tiptap); [Hocuspocus](https://github.com/ueberdosis/hocuspocus)). BlockNote gets to a Notion-like UX faster but its `xl-*` packages are GPL-3.0 or commercial ([BlockNote](https://github.com/TypeCellOS/BlockNote)). If the team would rather not build an editor at all, Outline is the fallback, with its Markdown MCP tools as the agent surface and a nightly markdown export for diffing and re-indexing. Obsidian stays as an optional client pointed at the same repository.

The review loop in front of AI drafts is the part no open project has fully built for multi-user wikis, but the pattern is consistent across the sources: ReadMe's guidance is that "a review workflow that works treats AI output as a proposal, not a commit" and that "auto-publishing drafts is what erodes trust" ([ReadMe](https://readme.com/resources/ai-writer-review-workflow)); Spotify's internal Backstage wiki pages have a member "accept, reject, or dismiss each suggestion after comparing it against the cited resources" ([Backstage docs](https://backstage.spotify.com/docs/xirp/workspaces/wiki-pages)); and Wikipedia's experience of being "flooded non-stop with horrendous drafts" ended in a March 2026 policy prohibiting LLM-generated article text ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence_in_Wikimedia_projects); [Beutler Ink](https://beutlerink.com/blog/wikiwise/crack-down-on-ai)). Concretely: the ingest agent emits a change plan, writes each proposed page as a draft with `status: proposed` and chunk-level `sources`, a review UI shows the diff beside the cited passages, acceptance sets `reviewed_by`, and a human edit sets `human_edited: true` so later regenerations propose rather than overwrite. The chat index should include only published pages so the assistant never cites an unreviewed draft, and index updates should follow llmwiki's ordering, "writes go to the source of truth first, then the search index updates" ([llmwiki](https://github.com/lucasastorian/llmwiki)), gated by a content hash so a typo fix does not re-embed a handbook ([kapa.ai](https://www.kapa.ai/library/how-to-keep-a-rag-knowledge-base-in-sync-with-changing-docs)), with a nightly reconciliation sweep to purge orphans.

## Postgres handles the vectors; GPUs belong on ingest, rerank and generation

The vector-database question dissolves once the scale is quantified. Ten thousand to one hundred thousand engineering PDFs at 20–50 chunks each is **0.5–5 million vectors**, and Qdrant's own benchmark notes that its largest set, one million 1536-dimensional vectors, "needs roughly 8.6 GB of RAM including vectors and index" ([Qdrant benchmark](https://qdrant.tech/benchmarks/single-node-speed-benchmark/)). At one million vectors every serious engine returns in single-digit milliseconds on CPU: one 2026 comparison measured Qdrant with scalar quantization at 6.8 ms p95 and 1,240 requests per second ahead of Milvus and pgvector ([DEV Community](https://dev.to/locionic/vector-databases-for-production-rag-2026-pinecone-vs-qdrant-vs-milvus-vs-pgvector-4fim)), and another found Milvus, Weaviate, Qdrant and pgvector all between 3 and 7 ms p95 ([Salt Techno](https://www.salttechno.ai/datasets/vector-database-performance-benchmark-2026/)). ANN latency is therefore never the bottleneck of a RAG pipeline; LLM generation is, by two to three orders of magnitude. The decision is about filtered search quality, hybrid support and operations, not raw speed.

Plain PostgreSQL with pgvector 0.8 is adequate and operationally simplest. Its 0.8.0 iterative index scans fixed the historical collapse of filtered queries, where a 10%-selective filter at default `ef_search=40` left "only 4 rows" on average ([ClickHouse](https://clickhouse.com/resources/engineering/scale-vector-search-postgres); [pgvector](https://github.com/pgvector/pgvector)). Adding **pgvectorscale** (PostgreSQL license) brings a StreamingDiskANN index with SBQ compression and label filtering, and TigerData's vendor test on 50 million 768-dimensional vectors at 99% recall reported 471 QPS against Qdrant's 41, while conceding that Qdrant won p95 latency (36.7 ms vs 60.4 ms) and index build time (3.3 h vs 11.1 h) ([pgvectorscale](https://github.com/timescale/pgvectorscale); [TigerData](https://www.tigerdata.com/blog/pgvector-vs-qdrant)). Qdrant's advantage is filtering during graph traversal: a third-party test on 100k vectors at 10% selectivity measured filtered recall@10 of 0.42 for default pgvector versus 1.00 for Qdrant ([pgContext](https://pgxn.org/dist/pgContext/docs/benchmarks/pgvector.html)). The practical rule is: if Postgres is already the system of record for the wiki, ACLs, chunks and queue, keep vectors there, enforce permissions as a `WHERE` clause with a btree on the tenant or space column, and size RAM so the HNSW graph never spills, because performance "falls off a cliff" once it does ([ClickHouse](https://clickhouse.com/resources/engineering/scale-vector-search-postgres)). Add Qdrant (Apache-2.0, one Rust container) only if heavy metadata filtering, native BM25-sparse fusion in one call, binary quantization or ColPali multi-vectors become necessary ([Qdrant hybrid docs](https://qdrant.tech/documentation/search-tuning/hybrid-search/)). Milvus, Weaviate and Vespa are justified only above roughly 100 million vectors.

Lexical search matters as much as dense search for engineering text, and Postgres needs help there. Native `tsvector` ranking "is not BM25: no term-frequency saturation, no document-length normalization", which is why **ParadeDB's pg_search** (AGPL-3.0 community edition) adds a real BM25 index with tokenizers, highlighting and hybrid search inside the same transaction ([ParadeDB](https://github.com/paradedb/paradedb)). The evidence that this channel is essential comes from the closest available proxy to specifications, text-and-table financial filings: BM25 alone (Recall@5 0.644) beat state-of-the-art dense retrieval (0.587), RRF hybrid reached 0.695, and hybrid plus a neural reranker reached **0.816**, with the largest gains on table-heavy questions ([arXiv 2604.01733](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.01733v1)). Clause numbers like "IEC 61508-2 §7.4.4", part codes and unit strings are exactly the tokens dense models blur. GraphRAG, by contrast, earns its roughly 1,000x indexing cost only on multi-hop "summarize themes" questions; a neutral academic comparison found plain RAG slightly ahead on single-hop factual lookup and "no single winner" ([arXiv 2502.11371](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.11371)), and Microsoft's own LazyGraphRAG brings indexing cost back to parity with vector RAG ([Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/blog/lazygraphrag/)). A typical engineer's query ("maximum operating temperature of part X per spec Y rev C") is single-hop. Prefer a lightweight explicit metadata graph (document → references → clause → tag) stored as Postgres rows over LLM-extracted entity graphs.

The CPU-versus-GPU question splits by pipeline stage, and the numbers are decisive.

| Stage | CPU | GPU | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| PDF parsing (layout, OCR, tables) | Docling median 0.79 s/page on x86 (~1.3 pg/s) | 114 ms/page on an L4 (~9 pg/s); OCR 8x, layout 14x faster ([Docling paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.17887)) | GPU for the bulk pass |
| Bulk embedding at ingest | bge-m3 ONNX ~0.86 s per long document on x86 ([bge-m3-onnx](https://huggingface.co/a-ivanovitch/bge-m3-onnx)) | bge-m3 at 512 tokens: ~277 texts/s on L40S, ~1,345 on H200 under TEI ([RunPod](https://www.runpod.io/blog/gpu-embedding-workloads-benchmark)) | GPU (10–100x) |
| Query embedding | 20–80 ms for a 0.3–0.6B model | 10–30 ms | CPU is fine |
| ANN search, 1–10M vectors | 3–10 ms p95 | Only index build benefits: 10M-vector HNSW build 8.5 h CPU vs 36.8 min on A10G ([OpenSearch](https://opensearch.org/?p=4066)) | CPU |
| Cross-encoder reranking | 278M-param reranker 25 pairs/s (~2 s for 50 candidates) | 748 pairs/s on RTX 4090 (<0.1 s for 50) ([EnsembleLink](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.21138)) | GPU if latency matters at concurrency |
| LLM generation | 70B Q4 at 3–5 tok/s on dual-Xeon ([InsiderLLM](https://insiderllm.com/pdfs/cpu-only-llms-what-actually-works.pdf)) | 8B Q4 at 128 tok/s on RTX 4090, 70B at 25 tok/s on H100 PCIe ([llama.cpp benchmarks](https://github.com/XiongjieDai/GPU-Benchmarks-on-LLM-Inference)); 70B FP8 ~1,850 tok/s aggregate on one H100 under vLLM ([Spheron](https://www.spheron.network/blog/vllm-vs-tensorrt-llm-vs-sglang-benchmarks)) | GPU, non-negotiable for interactive use |

So the vector database is CPU-based and the GPU budget is driven by parsing, reranking and generation. One more serving fact matters: Red Hat's single-A100 test found vLLM peaking at **793 tok/s** while Ollama, even tuned to 32 parallel slots, stayed flat at 41 tok/s because it serializes requests by default ([Red Hat](https://developers.redhat.com/articles/2025/08/08/ollama-vs-vllm-deep-dive-performance-benchmarking)); Ollama is a development tool, not a multi-user server.

For models, the open-weight leaders are the **Qwen3-Embedding** family (0.6B/4B/8B, Apache-2.0, 32K context, Matryoshka dimensions, MTEB Multilingual 64.33/69.45/70.58) with companion Qwen3-Rerankers, all served by Hugging Face TEI ([Qwen3-Embedding](https://github.com/QwenLM/Qwen3-Embedding); [TEI](https://github.com/huggingface/text-embeddings-inference)). On BEIR, Qwen3-Reranker-8B scores 61.81 and 4B 59.92 against bge-reranker-v2-m3 at 56.42 ([Querit-Reranker paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.19037)); jina-reranker-v3 scores slightly higher but its weights are CC-BY-NC, which rules out commercial self-hosting ([ianas.fr](https://ianas.fr/en/blog/2026/06/07/reranker-comparatif-cohere-bge-jina-voyage/)). BGE-M3 remains unique in emitting dense, sparse and multi-vector outputs in one pass if you want lexical signals from the same model. For drawings, scanned legacy specs and figure-heavy datasheets, ColQwen-class visual retrievers score about 89 nDCG@5 on ViDoRe v1 but store 1,030 patch vectors per page unless pooled, and tables are their relative weak spot (ColPali 58.6 on tables vs 84.5 on visual content) ([ColPali](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.01449); [REAL-MM-RAG](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.12342v1)); run them as a parallel index for drawings, not as the primary path for text specs.

Hardware falls into three tiers. A single workstation with an RTX 5090 (32 GB) or 4090 (24 GB), 64–128 GB RAM and a few TB of NVMe serves roughly ten concurrent users with an 8–14B model at 100–200 tok/s single-stream, with TEI embeddings and reranker sharing the GPU in under 3 GB of VRAM, and the entire vector set in under 5 GB of RAM. A small on-prem server with one 80–96 GB GPU (H100/H200, or RTX 6000 Pro Blackwell 96 GB renting at $1.19–1.85/hr ([Kaitchup](https://kaitchup.substack.com/p/faster-and-cheaper-than-the-h100))) runs a 70B-class FP8 model for 50–70 concurrent generations. Beyond that, either add GPU nodes or, more cheaply below roughly 10–20 million generated tokens per day, call a hosted API while keeping embeddings, ANN and reranking local. H100 rentals ranged $2.89–$11.06/hr across providers in September 2026 ([thundercompute](https://thundercompute.com/blog/ai-gpu-rental-market-trends)).

## Parsing and chunking decide answer quality more than the model does

For engineering PDFs, the parsing layer is where the value is, and the open benchmarks split it into two tiers. Classic layout-model pipelines run on CPU but score 50–86; small "expert VLM" parsers need a GPU and score 90–96, beating frontier general models. On the OmniDocBench v1.6 leaderboard, **PaddleOCR-VL-1.6 scores 96.34 overall with table TEDS 94.76** and **MinerU2.5-Pro 95.75 with TEDS 93.42 and formula CDM 97.45**, against Gemini 3 Pro at 92.91, GPT-5.2 at 86.59, the classic MinerU pipeline at 86.47 (TEDS 81.88) and Marker at 78.44 (TEDS 65.77) ([OmniDocBench](https://github.com/opendatalab/OmniDocBench)). On Ai2's olmOCR-bench the ordering differs (Chandra 83.1, olmOCR 82.4, PaddleOCR-VL 80.0, Marker 76.1, MinerU 75.2, Mistral OCR API 72.0) ([olmOCR](https://github.com/allenai/olmocr)), and Datalab's own run puts Marker balanced at 2.9 pages/s and Docling at 2.1 pages/s on a B200 host versus the MinerU pipeline at 0.54 ([Marker README](https://github.com/datalab-to/marker)). Every figure is from the benchmark owner or the tool vendor, Docling has no row on OmniDocBench, and no benchmark covers requirement clauses, units or multi-column standards, so the team must run its own 50–100 page sample before committing.

The licensing of the parsers is as important as their scores. Docling is MIT and now a Linux Foundation project with a VLM pipeline, `docling-serve` API and MCP server, and it handles DOCX, PPTX, XLSX, HTML and images as well as PDF ([Docling](https://github.com/docling-project/docling)). MinerU is "Apache 2.0 with additional conditions", with a GPU tier needing at least 8 GB VRAM and CPU tiers at 2–8 GB RAM ([MinerU](https://github.com/opendatalab/MinerU)). Marker's code is Apache-2.0 but its model weights are free only for research, personal use and organisations under $5M in funding or revenue ([Marker](https://github.com/datalab-to/marker)). PyMuPDF and pymupdf4llm are AGPL or commercial, and the AGPL network-use clause bites a hosted platform ([PyMuPDF](https://pymupdf.readthedocs.io/en/latest/_sources/about.rst)). The recommended default is a GPU-hosted expert VLM (MinerU2.5 via vLLM or PaddleOCR-VL) as the primary parser for anything with tables, formulas or scans, Docling as the glue layer and canonical document schema for Office formats and born-digital prose, and frontier general VLMs reserved for pages the pipeline flags as low-confidence, for diagrams and photos, and for semantic extraction into structured fields, because on pure transcription they no longer beat the sub-2B open models. Hallucinated field values are the documented risk of VLM extraction, with one invoice team reporting Gemini inventing a VAT ID "that's nowhere in the document" ([LinkedIn, Schillmeier](https://cy.linkedin.com/in/christoph-schillmeier)), so pair every VLM extraction with a check that the value appears in the OCR text.

Throughput sets the ingest plan. For 10,000 documents averaging 30 pages, a single 16-thread CPU server running Docling or Marker-fast at 0.3–1.3 pages/s needs 64–280 hours for layout-aware parsing, while one L4/4090-class GPU at 2–9 pages/s finishes in 9–42 hours ([Docling paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.17887); [Marker README](https://github.com/datalab-to/marker)). Hosted alternatives for the same 300,000 pages range from roughly $30–60 via olmOCR on DeepInfra or Cirrascale ([olmOCR](https://github.com/allenai/olmocr)) to about $300 for Mistral OCR 3 batch at $1 per 1,000 pages ([InfoQ](https://www.infoq.com/news/2026/01/mistral-ocr3)) to thousands for Azure Document Intelligence Layout or Reducto. Run two passes: a fast text-layer pass on day one so search works immediately, then a GPU VLM pass prioritized by document class.

Chunking should follow the document's own structure. Anthropic's Contextual Retrieval showed that prepending a 50–100 token generated context to each chunk cut top-20 retrieval failures from 5.7% to 3.7%, to 2.9% with contextual BM25, and to **1.9% with reranking**, at a one-time cost of about $1.02 per million document tokens with prompt caching, and that retrieving ~150 candidates and reranking to 20 beat passing 5 or 10 chunks ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/contextual-retrieval)). An oil-and-gas study of enterprise documents found structure-aware chunking gave the best retrieval effectiveness at lower compute than breakpoint semantic chunking, while all four strategies "demonstrated limited effectiveness on P&IDs" ([arXiv 2603.24556](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.24556)). For specifications and standards the recipe is: parse to a structured model with heading levels and table cells, chunk at the numbered clause so each "shall" statement carries its clause ID, keep every table atomic with its caption and heading path, prepend a context line with document number, revision and section path, index in BM25 and dense, then rerank.

Revisions need their own design because similarity ranking cannot tell authoritative from stale. VersionRAG argues "even minor version changes can fundamentally alter the validity of an answer" ([VersionRAG](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.08109)), and practitioner guidance is to separate a document's stable identity from its revision, give every chunk a state (active, superseded, draft, archived), filter default retrieval to active before similarity scoring, and retire rather than delete superseded content because audits need it ([ZimaSpace](https://shop.zimaspace.com/blogs/tech-ai-hub/features-reliable-document-version-selection-rag)). Mandatory chunk metadata is therefore `document_id`, `revision`, `status`, `effective_date`, `page`, `bbox` and `section_path`, with a `current_revision` pointer per document and a structured clause-level diff between revisions that feeds only the changed parts to the wiki-update step. Standards get the same treatment with edition year.

Drawings and P&IDs are a different modality and text parsers are the wrong tool. In the BLUEPRINT study of a 1,500-file engineering archive, a CLIP classifier separated drawings from documents at ~93% while text-only BART reached ~49% with 0.8% recall on drawings ([arXiv 2602.13345](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.13345)); a 2026 P&ID compliance paper that extracted data from DXF and confined the LLM to code generation reached **97.5% F1 against 55.9%** for an image-based LLM baseline, warning that direct LLM application "raises serious concerns, including hallucination and non-determinism" ([EC3 2026](https://ec-3.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/EC32026_260.pdf)). Where DEXPI data exists, **pyDEXPI** (AGPL-3.0, 235 stars) loads DEXPI 1.3 into Pydantic classes and NetworkX graphs ([pyDEXPI](https://github.com/process-intelligence-research/pyDEXPI)). Equipment tags should be extracted by site-specific regex families validated against the master tag list, and VLM-read tags on drawings accepted only when they match a known tag or a human confirms them.

## Compliance checking needs a deterministic judge around the LLM

The accuracy evidence for clause-level compliance verdicts is sobering and consistent. In CTRAG, GPT-4's precision ranged from 71.9% down to 62.1% and recall from 85.3% to 72.0% as chunks grew, and "GPT-4o and Gemini Flash miss most Non-Compliant cases without further guidance" ([arXiv 2608.02472](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.02472)). A human-in-the-loop construction compliance study reported clause extraction at precision 0.50–1.00, recall 0.70–1.00 and F1 0.67–0.93, concluding that compliance decisions still need deterministic logic for traceable, reproducible results ([Advanced Engineering Informatics](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1474034626008049)). Agentic extraction of atomic requirements from unstructured specs achieved exact semantic match for only 43.6% of generated requirements and 60.6% recall of ground truth ([arXiv 2604.18228](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.18228)). A hybrid of formal logic and GPT-3 for contradiction detection on a real electric-bus project hit 99% accuracy but 60% recall ([Design Society 2024](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/C7D7E69A58BBC2BC6054AF0BB972A3E1/S2732527X24002074a.pdf/towards-an-automatic-contradiction-detection-in-requirements-engineering.pdf)). And a September 2026 benchmark of ten OpenAI and Anthropic models judging requirement quality against INCOSE criteria found false-alarm medians of 0.67 for conformance and 0.33 for ambiguity but only 0.09 for completeness, with models reliable on linguistically explicit issues and poor on judgment-based ones such as correctness and necessity, concluding they are "not suitable for autonomous quality evaluation" but can assist experts ([arXiv 2609.03230](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.03230)). The systematic failure mode across all of these is missed non-compliance, the false "compliant".

The architecture that falls out of this is a staged pipeline, and the open prototypes already sketch it. Parse both documents deterministically and extract atomic requirements with human curation; pair each requirement with candidate evidence by hybrid retrieval, chunking datasheet tables row by row as CompChecker does; have the LLM emit a schema-constrained verdict with mandatory verbatim quotes; verify every quote programmatically against the source text as ctsm does; parse numeric values with units and tolerances into typed structures and compare them in code (unit conversion, not prose), reserving the LLM for text-only clauses; run reverse checks so every obligation in the spec is accounted for; and route any "insufficient evidence" or unverified verdict to a human queue with a coverage dashboard of requirements that have no evidence ([ctsm](https://github.com/epifaneia/ctsm); [CompChecker](https://github.com/Aaryan-Chokshi/CompChecker)). Because false-alarm rates vary sevenfold by criterion, thresholds and human-review routing should be per rule, not global. The primary metric to optimize and report is false "compliant" verdicts, which ctsm holds at zero on its small set. Multi-agent debate, as in AICCE's structured-debate resolution when section agents disagree ([arXiv 2604.03330](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.03330)), is worth reserving for uncertain cases given that agentic loops multiply token spend several-fold.

Helping engineers write good specifications is a rule-checking problem with a well-codified rulebook. The INCOSE Guide to Writing Requirements v4 (1 July 2023) defines characteristics C1–C14 and rules grouped by quality focus, such as complete sentences, active voice, and no vague terms or escape clauses ([INCOSE GtWR v4 summary](https://www.incose.org/docs/default-source/working-groups/requirements-wg/guidetowritingrequirements/incose_rwg_gtwr_v4_summary_sheet.pdf)), and EARS (Rolls-Royce, RE'09) gives five syntactic patterns for well-formed requirements ([University of Manchester](https://research.manchester.ac.uk/en/publications/easy-approach-to-requirements-syntax-ears/)). Every commercial assistant is essentially a rule-checker against these, and the 2026 benchmark says to implement them as explicit named rules and flag necessity and correctness for human review rather than auto-scoring. For equipment specifications there is no verified LLM datasheet generator; the viable path is template-driven generation from a property schema per equipment class, filled from the project knowledge base, with the LLM writing prose and rationale and each parameter cross-referenced to a governing clause by identifier. The open dictionaries for those schemas exist: the IEC Common Data Dictionary (IEC 61360 model, IEC 62720 units) ([IEC CDD](https://tc3.iec.ch/tc-activity/common-data-dictionary-cdd/)), the IDTA "Generic Frame for Technical Data" and Digital Nameplate submodels with ECLASS semantics under CC-BY-4.0 ([IDTA 02003](https://industrialdigitaltwin.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/IDTA-02003_Generic-Frame-for-Technical-Data.pdf); [IDTA 02006](https://industrialdigitaltwin.org/en/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2025/10/IDTA-02006-3-0-1_Submodel_Digital-Nameplate.pdf)), the POSC Caesar reference data service with SPARQL endpoints cross-referencing CFIHOS and DEXPI ([PCA RDS](https://rds.posccaesar.org/features/)), and ISO 15926-6:2024, confirmed current in 2026 ([ISO](https://www.iso.org/standard/84301.html)). For systems integration across disciplines, **SysML v2** (adopted by OMG 30 June 2025, with spec PDFs updated March 2026 for ISO submission) gives first-class typed requirements, `interface def`, `connection def` and verification cases ([SysML-v2-Release](https://github.com/Systems-Modeling/SysML-v2-Release)), and cross-discipline conflicts such as electrical load versus heat rejection are tractable as typed-quantity rules over a knowledge graph, which no published plant-wide system yet does. Interchange should start with ReqIF 1.2 import/export with per-tool profiles, since round-tripping is lossy and tool-flavoured ([StrictDoc FAQ](https://strictdoc.readthedocs.io/en/latest/strictdoc_02_faq.html)), plus Excel/CSV compliance matrices and `needs.json`, with OSLC RM 2.1 as a later live-link option ([OASIS](https://www.oasis-open.org/?p=5068)).

The legal constraint on ingesting standards is the single most important non-technical finding. US courts have found *non-commercial* republication of standards *incorporated by reference into law* to be fair use, most recently the Third Circuit's precedential April 2026 decision in ASTM v. UpCodes ([LawNext](https://www.lawnext.com/2026/04/third-circuit-rules-that-upcodes-publication-of-incorporated-building-standards-is-likely-fair-use.html)), but that does not reach commercial use or the ASME, API and IEC texts that are mostly not incorporated into law. Contract terms are moving explicitly against AI: ISO's Online Browsing Platform terms bar training or fine-tuning language or generative-AI models on ISO content and opt out of the EU text-and-data-mining exception ([ISO OBP Terms](https://www.iso.org/home/store/iso-obp-terms.html)); IEEE licences prohibit using licensed content to develop or train AI tools and storing it within any AI tool ([IEEE API Terms](https://developer.ieee.org/API_Terms_of_Use2)); ASHRAE "prohibits the entry of content from any ASHRAE publication... into any AI tool" ([ASHRAE](https://www.ashrae.org/technical-resources/bookstore/ashrae-digital-collections)); and SFS bans even "using generative AI tools to analyse standards" ([SFS](https://store.sfs.fi/en/copyright)). ASME and API terms could not be retrieved. The defensible design is "reference, don't redistribute": store standard ID, edition, clause number and clause title (facts), the user's own structured parameter extractions, and pointers; process the user's licensed copy ephemerally at check time under the user's own licence; and use CC-BY and open dictionaries as the semantic layer. Whether user-side LLM processing of licensed text is itself permitted under the ASHRAE/SFS wording is an unresolved question for counsel.

## Build thin on eight components, in five phases

The build-versus-build-on decision resolves against forking. The requested product's core is a reviewed wiki plus structured compliance checks, and no turnkey platform has either; Onyx, RAGFlow and WeKnora each ship a fixed, heavy service topology (Onyx alone: FastAPI, Celery, Next.js, Postgres, Redis, OpenSearch with a 2 GB JVM heap, two model servers, nginx, object store ([compose](https://github.com/onyx-dot-app/onyx/blob/main/deployment/docker_compose/docker-compose.yml))) and needs roughly 2 GB of RAM per GB of source documents at small corpus sizes ([Onyx resourcing](https://docs.onyx.app/deployment/getting_started/resourcing.md)). The documented regret runs the other way: Octomind removed LangChain after twelve months in production and shipped faster with simpler building blocks ([Octomind](https://octomind.dev/blog/why-we-no-longer-use-langchain-for-building-our-ai-agents)), and Anthropic's own guidance is to "begin with LLM APIs directly" because frameworks "hide the underlying prompts and responses, which makes debugging harder" ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents)). Build regret is real only for teams that also write their own parsers, embedding servers or inference engines; composing Docling, TEI, vLLM, Postgres and Hocuspocus with a few thousand lines of glue is what Onyx itself did. WeKnora deserves one specific role: install it (and Onyx) in week one as a reference and benchmark to calibrate retrieval quality and UX expectations, and borrow its wiki-with-rollback and jump-to-passage patterns rather than its code. If the team is one or two people and must ship something in weeks, WeKnora is the one platform worth adopting as-is for the ingest-chat-wiki slice while the compliance engine is built beside it against its REST API; the cost is living with a fast-moving MIT codebase you do not control.

The recommended stack is deliberately boring at the core. PostgreSQL holds documents, chunks, vectors (pgvector 0.8 with `halfvec`, plus pgvectorscale), BM25 (ParadeDB, or tsvector plus RRF if AGPL is a problem), wiki page metadata, ACLs and the job queue, using **Hatchet** (MIT, Postgres-backed, Python/TypeScript/Go SDKs, DAG workflows, load-tested to 10k tasks/s ([Hatchet](https://github.com/hatchet-dev/hatchet))) or pg-boss for a Node team. The API tier is Python (FastAPI or Litestar) with **Pydantic AI** as the only agent layer (MIT, typed, MCP in core, OpenTelemetry-native ([pydantic-ai](https://github.com/pydantic/pydantic-ai))), or TypeScript on Hono/Bun with Mastra; the benchmark evidence says the API tier for 50–200 engineers "runs equally well on FastAPI, Express, or Go" ([index.dev](https://www.index.dev/skill-vs-skill/backend-go-vs-nodejs-vs-python-fastapi)), so pick by who maintains it and keep every CPU-bound stage in workers or dedicated servers so the GIL never matters. Serving is **TEI** for Qwen3-Embedding and Qwen3-Reranker and **vLLM or SGLang** for any local LLM (SGLang wins by up to 29% on prefix-heavy RAG traffic, otherwise within ~5% ([PremAI](https://www.premai.io/blog/vllm-vs-sglang-vs-lmdeploy-fastest-llm-inference-engine-in-2026/))). The front end is React/Next.js or SvelteKit with Tiptap and Hocuspocus. Deployment is Docker Compose on one GPU host until there are multiple GPU nodes or SLO-driven canaries ([kunalganglani](https://www.kunalganglani.com/blog/docker-compose-kubernetes-ai-ml-2026)). Observability is Langfuse (MIT core, self-hosting free and uncapped ([Langfuse](https://github.com/langfuse/langfuse))) via OpenTelemetry; evals are DeepEval or Ragas plus promptfoo in CI on a golden set from day one; SSO is Authentik for a small team (MIT core, can proxy-protect tools with no login of their own ([Authentik](https://github.com/goauthentik/authentik))) or Keycloak if the organisation already runs it. Object storage should not default to MinIO community binaries after the April 2026 archival; use the cloud's S3 or another S3-compatible store behind a thin abstraction.

Generation is a cost and residency decision, not a capability one. With 200 engineers at 20 queries a day and ~16k input tokens per query, the research's own cost model lands at roughly $150 per day on Claude Sonnet 5.5 before caching, about $7.50 per day on Haiku 5.5 (list $0.10/$0.50 per million tokens for prompts under 100k) and about $11 per day for gpt-oss-120b on Groq-class hosts, all below the cost of one rented GPU ([Anthropic pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing)). Anthropic enables zero-data-retention per organization, while its "Covered Models" (Fable 5.1, Mythos 5.1, Fable 5, Mythos 5) require 30-day retention and are not ZDR-eligible unless authorized, and on Bedrock and Vertex the cloud provider is the data processor ([Anthropic retention](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/manage-claude/api-and-data-retention)). So: default chat on Haiku-class or a hosted open model with caching, compliance checks against whole standards on Sonnet 5.5's flat-rate 1M context with citations, and fully local vLLM on a 80–96 GB GPU (Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, Qwen3.8-27B or gpt-oss-120b) only where documents cannot leave the premises. Embeddings and rerankers are self-hosted regardless, because every chunk and every query passes through them.

The prioritized roadmap below orders work by measured impact per unit of effort, with the evidence that justifies each step.

The gaps are material and should shape how the first months are run. No independent latency or throughput benchmark exists for any candidate platform at tens of thousands of documents; WeKnora's 40k-document claim and Onyx's 220k-document benchmark are self-reported, and RAGFlow's generated wiki may not be human-editable at all (the docs page was unreachable). No retrieval or parsing benchmark covers engineering specifications, standards or datasheets, so the team's own labelled 50–100 page sample and a golden question set are the only way to choose parsers and tune chunking. No open project implements the full ingest → proposal → review → publish → re-index loop for a multi-user wiki, and no data exists on reviewer throughput or acceptance rates for AI-drafted engineering pages. No published benchmark addresses LLM handling of units and tolerances in compliance checking. ASME and API licence terms on AI processing were not retrievable, and the broad ASHRAE/SFS wording may prohibit even user-side processing. Several facts rest on vendor blogs or search excerpts because the research sandbox could not reach arxiv.org, huggingface.co, qdrant.tech and most vendor documentation; the pgvector-versus-Qdrant numbers in particular come from each vendor's own test. Obsidian Bases, Outline/Docmost/BookStack performance at 10k+ pages, and the current CFIHOS and DEXPI release status were not verified.

## Conclusion

The most useful reframing from this research is that "Engineering Brain" is not a RAG product with extras; it is a document-control and review system in which retrieval is one component. The platforms that dominate the stars (RAGFlow, Dify, Open WebUI) optimize for the chat window, and the places where an engineering team will actually win or lose, parsing of tables and clauses, revision-aware retrieval, a review gate in front of machine-written pages, and deterministic verdicts with verbatim evidence, are exactly the places none of them invest. That is why the economics favor a thin custom build on Postgres, Docling/MinerU, TEI, vLLM and Tiptap/Hocuspocus, and why the Karpathy-style markdown wiki survives as the canonical store even though its file-and-git ergonomics must be hidden behind a web editor for non-software engineers.

Two consequences follow for how to start. First, the GPU purchase should be justified by ingest parsing and reranking rather than by generation, which can be rented per token at a fraction of a GPU's cost while the corpus is small; buying for local generation only makes sense when a residency mandate or token volume demands it. Second, the hardest long-term risk is not technical but contractual: standards bodies are writing anti-AI clauses into licences faster than courts are expanding fair use, so a knowledge base that stores clause identifiers and the organisation's own extracted parameters, and processes licensed text only ephemerally under the user's licence, is the design most likely to still be legal in three years.
